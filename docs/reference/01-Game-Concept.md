# Failure is Not an Option

*A 2D NASA flight director simulator and narrative strategy game, framed from the director's first-person viewpoint.*

**Concept brief — consolidated version, revised for the user's 2D art decision.**

The central experience is the responsibility of command: you read the uncertainty in a controller's assessment, decide whose judgment to trust, and live with the consequences twenty missions later. The space program remembers your decisions.

**Failure is Not an Option** spans Gemini through the Artemis era. You assemble crews, lead Mission Control, negotiate the future of NASA, and create an alternate history through accumulated decisions.

You might establish a lunar settlement in the 1970s. You might build an international space program that survives political upheaval. You might become the most powerful person at NASA—or leave your office carrying a cardboard box and a vest nobody will see on console again.

## The director and the vest

Your default character is **Glen Kurtz**, an unmistakable fictional homage to Gene Kranz: horn-rimmed glasses, a crew cut or regulation haircut, and the vest. Players can rename the director and choose male, female, or nonbinary identity, with appearance selected independently.

The director remains ageless. Colleagues age, astronauts retire, administrations change, and Mission Control transforms around you. The game accepts your continuity as its central fiction.

Every mission begins with a new vest. Completing a scenario earns its patch, which players can place on any vest in their collection. The wardrobe becomes a campaign archive: inspect a patch to revisit the crew, decisions, outcome, and historical divergence behind it.

A successful rescue deserves a patch even when the original objective was abandoned. Personalization should celebrate the player's history without turning clothing into arbitrary engineering bonuses. The vest is non-negotiable.

First person still leaves room for character expression: dressing-room mirrors, your hands adjusting the headset, your vest visible when you look down, and illustrated newspaper portraits after major missions.

## What you actually do

Each mission follows six connected stages:

1. **Set the objective.** Examine the mission brief, political expectations, technical readiness, and unresolved problems inherited from earlier flights.
2. **Build the team.** Select astronauts, backups, flight controllers, and specialist support. Allocate training and establish contingency procedures.
3. **Work the room.** Speak privately with engineers through on-screen conversations, examine disagreements, and decide which concerns require investigation.
4. **Take the console.** Monitor telemetry, request analysis, consult specialists, and authorize decisions as the mission unfolds.
5. **Account for the outcome.** Conduct the debrief, explain decisions publicly, and respond to investigations or recognition.
6. **Shape the next mission.** Spend funding, negotiate partnerships, revise procedures, and decide what NASA attempts next.

The central experience is an illustrated view from the flight director's console. Clickable areas and scene transitions lead to illustrated briefing rooms, offices, corridors, and the viewing gallery. First person describes the viewpoint and role; the game uses 2D scenes rather than a navigable 3D environment.

During operations, clicking a station opens its controller's portrait, assessment, supporting evidence, and available questions. You give direction through the appropriate channels; astronauts retain onboard judgment, particularly when communications are unavailable.

Narrative play allows time to pause while reading. An optional pressure mode lets selected decision windows continue counting down.

## Make uncertainty playable

Borrow the branching conversations and spatial navigation of the supplied references, leaning toward Citizen Sleeper's menu approach, but give decisions an operational language and a distinctly NASA identity.

A choice should communicate:

- What you intend to achieve.
- Which evidence supports it.
- What resources or safety margins it consumes.
- What remains uncertain.

Instead of “Persuasion: 70%,” a controller might report on-screen: “The backup reading agrees, but both sensors share the same power supply.”

Preparation creates options. A specialist can identify a diagnostic path. Joint training can make a complicated procedure executable. Trust can make someone willing to challenge your assumption before it becomes an order.

Chance can represent imperfect equipment and incomplete information, but major consequences should have understandable causes. Afterward, the player should be able to trace what happened without feeling that the game secretly changed the rules.

## Crews are people with working relationships

Recruit from era-appropriate rosters based on real astronauts. Historical biographies establish experience; the game clearly identifies invented dialogue and relationship developments as dramatization.

Crew selection weighs piloting, rendezvous, engineering, EVA, science, and command experience. Equally important are fatigue, shared training, communication habits, and trust earned during your campaign.

Compatibility should emerge from events. Two astronauts who rehearse together become more coordinated. A commander whose concerns you repeatedly dismiss becomes less candid. A backup who finally receives an assignment remembers that opportunity.

There is no permanent “best crew.” Assigning every veteran to one flight leaves the next mission without experienced leadership. Training younger astronauts costs time now and creates options later.

Players also build the ground team. A brilliant controller who presents conclusions cautiously creates a different room from an equally capable controller who challenges you immediately.

## History supplies the starting conditions

The campaign includes famous missions, but lesser-known flights provide many of its best scenarios. These are proposed gameplay treatments of historical events; their alternate outcomes are fictional.

| Campaign chapter | Mission anchors | Central decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Gemini | Gemini VIII; Gemini IX-A | Diagnose conflicting evidence, develop docking capability, recognize human limits during EVA. |
| Apollo | Apollo 8, 12, 13, and 15 | Balance readiness, schedule pressure, rescue capability, and scientific ambition. |
| Living in space | Skylab 2 and subsequent expeditions | Repair damaged infrastructure, manage workload, and decide whether orbital habitation becomes permanent. |
| Opening the door | Apollo–Soyuz | Negotiate compatible systems, shared procedures, and the political meaning of cooperation. |
| Shuttle | STS-51-F, STS-49, Hubble servicing | Salvage objectives after failures, authorize unusual repairs, and defend program value. |
| Shared orbit | Shuttle–Mir and ISS assembly | Manage mutual dependence, partner commitments, station logistics, and competing priorities. |
| Commercial and Artemis era | Crew transport, lunar logistics, surface operations | Choose what NASA owns, what it purchases, and who controls access to the next frontier. |

Gemini VIII offers a particularly strong opening crisis: its first docking was followed by a thruster malfunction and mission abort. Gemini IX-A adds an obstructed docking target and difficult EVA experience. Sources: [NASA: Gemini VIII](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/gemini-viii/) and [NASA: Gemini IX](https://www.nasa.gov/history/gemini-ix-and-the-angry-alligator/).

Skylab's repair mission and STS-49's improvised satellite recovery offer equally strong stories about preparation and adaptation. Sources: [NASA: Skylab 2](https://www.nasa.gov/history/50-years-ago-skylab-2-the-we-fix-anything-crew/) and [NASA: STS-49](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/sts-49/).

Era chapters provide structure, but their missions adapt to the world you created. A campaign with a permanent lunar settlement should enter the Artemis chapter facing expansion and governance problems appropriate to that history. Later campaign scenarios are design proposals rather than claims about the completion or schedule of real future missions.

## Choices change the conditions of later choices

The campaign tracks both quantities—funding, technical readiness, institutional trust—and specific facts:

- You retained heavy-lift production.
- You established a common docking standard.
- You suppressed an engineering objection.
- You trained a generation of station crews.

Those facts unlock missions, alter costs, change relationships, and create consequences years later.

A possible alternate-history route is:

**Successful Apollo program → political support converted into sustained appropriations → heavy-lift production retained → cargo landers and habitation systems developed → repeated surface logistics demonstrated → occupied lunar outpost during the 1970s.**

A larger Moon colony is an ambitious extension of that branch. Money accelerates development, but the player must still solve power, shielding, life support, resupply, and evacuation.

Every achievement creates obligations. Your lunar settlement needs recurring launches. Keeping it supplied may consume the funding another campaign spends on Shuttle, science missions, or international cooperation.

The alternate history becomes convincing because its successes have maintenance costs.

## Influence, funding, and the danger of success

Keep three distinct forms of power:

- **Operational trust:** whether crews and controllers believe in your judgment.
- **Political capital:** whether decision-makers will back your proposals.
- **Formal authority:** what you are actually permitted to approve.

Successful missions generally strengthen the case for more funding, but the player must convert that success into support. A spectacular achievement can also create pressure to cut spending because politicians believe the objective is complete.

Early in the campaign, crew assignments and program investments require negotiation. Promotions progressively grant direct control. At the top, an exceptional mandate can give you overwhelming NASA Administrator-level authority while preserving the option to personally lead pivotal missions.

Poor performance can remove responsibilities, trigger probation, cause demotion, or end the career. Institutional collapse should also emerge from repeated failures of judgment, candor, or leadership—not simply from missing a numerical score.

“Success” includes protecting people and making sound abort decisions. Otherwise, the reward system would teach players to gamble with crews.

## International relationships have substance

The European agency reference is interpreted as **ESA**. Use organizations appropriate to each period: ESRO and ELDO before ESA's creation in 1975, Soviet counterparts during the Cold War, and Russian institutions and Roscosmos in later chapters. Source: [ESA institutional history](https://www.esa.int/About_Us/50_years_of_ESA/History_of_Europe_in_space).

Cooperation involves concrete exchanges: tracking coverage, docking standards, launch access, scientific instruments, rescue commitments, manufacturing, and crew seats. Apollo–Soyuz provides a historical foundation through its focus on docking compatibility and potential international rescue. Source: [NASA: Apollo–Soyuz overview](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo-soyuz-test-project-overview/).

Relationships should distinguish engineers, agency leadership, and governments. Your counterpart may trust you while their government blocks the agreement. A rival may share emergency assistance while refusing your long-term proposal.

Later chapters can introduce European, Russian, Japanese, Canadian, Indian, and other partners alongside commercial space companies. Contractor negotiations revolve around price, reliability, proprietary information, ownership, and dependence.

Your choices can strengthen diplomatic openings or intensify competition. Wider politics also exert independent pressure, so maintaining cooperation sometimes means protecting it through events beyond your control.

## Visual identity

Combine **2D comic-book realism, expressive ink work, restrained textures, and dramatic lighting**. Draw from the supplied references' strong silhouettes, selective detail, flat color, and illustrated portraits, grounded in recognizable NASA spaces and working materials.

Mission Control uses layered 2D illustrations with strong architectural shapes. Characters appear as expressive illustrated portraits with restrained animation or transitions. Detail concentrates on faces, hands, spacecraft diagrams, console labels, and embroidered patches. Foreground console elements, room illustrations, portraits, and light overlays are separate assets where useful. Limited parallax may suggest depth without changing the game into 3D.

Overhead fixtures are depicted casting pools of light across Mission Control, leaving shadows between consoles. Painted light and shadow establish the scene; animated 2D overlays can vary screen glow and indicator lights. Glowing screens illuminate faces, glasses, hands, and vest fabric. Strong contrasts preserve readable silhouettes and interface text.

The palette begins with institutional cream, desaturated console green, charcoal, paper white, and restrained NASA blue. Amber and red communicate operational conditions. Later eras introduce cleaner displays while retaining the same visual grammar.

Menus resemble flight-plan binders, personnel dossiers, telemetry plots, routing slips, and annotated procedures. Branching choices occupy the edge of your working view; evidence can be pinned beside them. Citizen Sleeper provides a reference for branching menu structure and navigation, while the visual vocabulary remains NASA's.

The game's newly created visual assets use an original fictional Flight Operations emblem rather than NASA's insignia, worm logotype, or seal. NASA remains the historical setting and may be named in ordinary text. Logos already present within original archival opening footage are preserved. The emblem concept and production distinction are recorded in [09-Original-Emblem-and-Archive-Policy.md](09-Original-Emblem-and-Archive-Policy.md).

## Soundscape and on-screen dialogue

**No dialogue is voiced, and no intelligible spoken lines are used. All dialogue appears on-screen with clear speaker identification.** This includes crew communications, controller assessments, private conversations, public statements, and political negotiations.

The soundscape uses ventilation hum, equipment fans, headset clicks, keyboard taps, paper movement, electronic alerts, and optional simulated indistinct background conversations. Background chatter never carries information the player needs to understand.

Atmosphere comes from the room: equipment continuing to run, an alert repeating, a headset connection opening, and the pause before the next on-screen message arrives. Tension depends on writing, message timing, portraits, lighting, and environmental sound.

## The first playable version

Build one complete Gemini VIII chapter: character creation, vest selection, a constrained crew and controller roster, preparation decisions, the mission crisis, and a debrief.

Then show a short playable opening to the next mission with changed staffing, procedures, and resources.

That second mission is essential evidence for the concept: the player must experience how an earlier decision changes what they can do next.
